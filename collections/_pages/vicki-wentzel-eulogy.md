title: Eulogy of Vicki Wentzel
description: "A eulogy of Vicki Wentzel, delivered at her memorial service on July 31, 2021, by her oldest son, Ryan Wentzel."
---
*I delivered the following eulogy at my mother's memorial service on July 31, 2021, at Grace Bible Church in Escondido, CA.*

---

I’m Ryan Wentzel, the older of Vicki and Monty’s two sons. We're so very grateful for your presence today. I count it to be one of the great privileges of my life to talk to you today about Vicki Wentzel, the woman I knew and loved as "mom."

Vicki Lynn Marcolongo Wentzel was born on October 15, 1955, in Trenton, NJ, to Robert and Dorothy Marcolongo. She was the second of five children, born between her older sister Donna and younger siblings Denise, Robert, and Kerri. She was also stepsister to Daniel, Stephen, and Katherine. The Marcolongo family resided in Levittown, PA for a time before moving to Willingboro, NJ. 

Mom graduated from John F. Kennedy High School in Willingboro in 1973. Not long afterward, she began dating and fell in love with a man named William Wentzel, Jr., better known as Monty. She once told me she described dad to others as tall, dark, and handsome. Vicki and Monty attended church together at Fountain of Life in Burlington, NJ, where they both developed a vibrant and lifelong faith in Jesus Christ. Over the years, they were involved in the church in various ways: mom taught children's Sunday School. Dad picked up kids for church on Sundays in a church-owned school bus. 

Vicki and Monty married on February 6, 1976, in Burlington, NJ. Both were babies, a mere 20 years old. They were blessed with two sons, Ryan in 1980 and Bradley in 1982. Dad described mom as the perfect mother. I know she would dispute that, but she really was an excellent mother. I can attest that it was a unique privilege to be her son, and I know Bradley would agree.

Mom and Dad built two houses together in NJ. They were a good team: dad had the construction skills, and mom was gifted at creating beautiful living spaces. In 1992, the Wentzel family moved to San Diego, CA. Eventually, mom and dad built a third home together on eight acres of land in Ramona.

Mom had a successful career working at a few different software and financial services companies before retiring about three years ago. She was loved by both her clients and coworkers, including software engineers. As a former software engineer, I can tell you that’s quite an accomplishment. It takes a special person to get along with us.

One of the greatest joys of her life was being grandmother, or Grammie as she was known, to eight beautiful grandchildren, six boys: Abraham, Benjamin, Bryce, Thomas, John, and Joshua; and two girls: Andi and Reece. The birthday cakes she baked for her grandchildren are legendary.

After a brief battle with cancer, mom finished her earthly pilgrimage, at home, with her devoted husband of 45 years by her side.

Giving a eulogy for someone like Vicki Wentzel is a daunting task, not because there’s little good that can be spoken, but because whatever I say this morning seems inadequate.

As I’ve spoken with mom’s family and friends over the past several days, there’s a word that many of you have used to describe her. The same word has been running through my mind. The word is “special.” Mom truly was a special person. While growing up, I assumed every mom was a Vicki Wentzel:

- Utterly devoted to her family, despite our weaknesses and faults. 
- Elegant, dignified, smart, strong, creative. 
- Silly. I know that seems to be the opposite of dignified, but she didn't take herself too seriously. Once you got her giggling, it was difficult for her to stop. The grandkids were especially skilled at sending her into a giggling fit. 
- Patient and long-suffering. More than once as a teenager, I came home hours after my midnight curfew, and inevitably I forgot my house key. Mom always answered the door, and she let me in instead of making me spend the night on the doorstep! She never yelled or scolded. She simply whispered, "Try to come home earlier next time."

It wasn’t until I grew up that I realized what a unique and beautiful gift she was to all of us.

Everyone loved mom: her siblings, her husband and sons, her daughters-in-law, her friends and neighbors, even people she had met met only a few times. People were drawn to her. Her physical beauty caught the eye. I’m sure that’s what first drew dad in. But her beauty wasn’t simply surface level. You could see it in her smile: it radiated genuine kindness and interest in others. Her gentle voice made you feel welcomed and loved. The beauty she created all around her—for example, in the way she decorated her home—was an overflow of her inner beauty as a person.

Her kindness, selflessness, and cheerfulness were all the more remarkable given the suffering she endured throughout her life. Mom was born with a congenital heart defect. She endured multiple heart surgeries as an infant, a child, and an adult. During her first year of marriage, she lost a kidney. Vicki loved children; she longed to be a mother. But her physical frailty seemed to preclude the possibility. Yet, she had two sons and poured all the love and compassion and wisdom she had into us. I didn’t fully grasp the seriousness of her health problems until adulthood because mom didn’t complain. She was grateful for the life the Lord gave her. The physical suffering she endured throughout her life sweetened her, rather than causing bitterness to dominate her character.

In recent months, all of us were surprised by her seemingly sudden sickness, and then, of course, her all-too-early death. As her health declined, and later when she was told that she had an incurable cancer, that she was in stage-four heart failure, that another disease was wreaking havoc on her lungs, that nothing more could be done for her, she never complained. She bore up under it with dignity and quiet strength. Of course, she expressed sadness about a few things: she wouldn’t get to attend the wedding of her oldest grandchild, Abraham, when the time came. Her youngest grandchild—cute, little Reece—wouldn’t remember her. Quite naturally, she experienced moments of fear. She wrestled with what her purpose was when she was largely confined to a recliner during the last weeks of her life. But she continued to entrust herself and her family to the God who had created and redeemed her. She told us she wasn’t angry. She had always wanted to be a wife and a mother, and the Lord had graciously fulfilled her desires. Again, she was grateful, even to the end.

Now, I don't want you to get the wrong idea about mom. She was a special woman, so incredibly special, but also a flawed and broken human being, like the rest of us, something she would readily acknowledge, something she would want you to recognize. She’d want you to understand that whatever kindness, gentleness, love, or strength you saw in her—and I think each of us could give plenty of examples—all of it was a small reflection of the radiant light of the Savior she loved and trusted. She would be upset today if we put all our focus on her and failed to marvel at the One who beautified her with his undeserved grace. Friends, the love you received from Vicki Wentzel was a little taste of the love her Savior had poured into her. She was one of God’s luminous gifts to us.

The Irish author Kevin Toolis has written that "Death is a universal occurrence with two options; you can do death well or you can do death badly." Mom did death well. Friends, I want you to know that Vicki Wentzel died the same way she lived: with quiet confidence and hope in her risen Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ. She lived the message of her favorite hymn, "It Is Well with My Soul." The first verse says, “When peace, like a river, attendeth my way, when sorrows like sea billows roll; whatever my lot, thou hast taught me to say, “It is well, it is well with my soul.”

Thanks be to God!
