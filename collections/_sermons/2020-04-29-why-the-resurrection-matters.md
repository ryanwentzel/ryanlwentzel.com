## Introduction

Many of us were taught a story in grade school about the honesty of George Washington. According to the story, the six year old Washington received a new hatchet as a gift. The first thing he did with it was shave off some of the bark from his father's prized cherry tree. As a result, the tree eventually died.

When the boy's father found out what happened, he became angry and confronted his son. The story recounts that young George, "looking at his father, with the sweet face of youth brightened with the inexpressible charm of all-conquering truth…bravely cried out, 'I can't tell a lie, Pa; you know I can't tell a lie. I did cut it with my hatchet'." The elder Washington was pleased with George's honesty and replied, "Such an act of heroism in my son is more worth than a thousand trees, though blossomed with silver, and their fruits of purest gold'."

As many of you know, the story's a myth. It was fabricated by Washington's first biographer a year after Washington's death. Now, Washington did, in fact, have a reputation for honesty. The biographer wanted to inspire his fellow citizens to emulate the character of their recently deceased President. But the story about six-year-old Washington and the cherry tree was just that, a story.

Does the fact that the story isn't true damage Washington's legacy? Does it change your standing as an American citizen? Does it erase the cultural and political heritage bequeathed to the nation by the Founding Fathers? Not in the least. It simply doesn't matter for the United States and its citizens whether Washington refused to tell a lie as a six year old.

What about the resurrection? Does it matter whether Christ was bodily raised from the dead? If he's still dead, does it change anything about the Christian faith? Does it threaten your identity as a Christian? Does it impact your hope for the future?

1 Corinthians 15 is one of the most extensive treatments of the resurrection in the New Testament. The section we're looking at today, verses 1 to 28, tells us why the resurrection of Christ matters. Some of the Christians in Corinth were confused about this point. They didn't directly deny that Christ had been raised. Instead they denied that believers would be raised. You can see that's the case in verse 12. Paul asks them, "how can some of you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?"

Perhaps these ex-pagan converts were still influenced by the worldview and philosophies in which they were raised. Many different ideas about death and the afterlife pervaded the ancient Greco-Roman world. Most people believed in some form of existence after death, but it wasn't a bodily existence. 

Many pitted the material against the spiritual. That which was material and physical was considered evil. Their hope was to discard the human body at death, setting the soul free from its prison.

The Greeks mocked the idea of bodily resurrection. It was repugnant to them. Recall Paul's preaching in Athens in Acts 17. As soon as he announced that Jesus rose from the dead, the listeners began to ridicule him (17:32).

Apparently the Corinthians thought there was no need for a bodily resurrection. They were already indwelt by the Holy Spirit. They were spiritually united to Christ. A return to bodily existence after death would be a step backwards. The spiritual is what really matters, not the physical.

Thing aren't that much different in our day, are they? Many, inside and outside the church, look forward to a disembodied future. It may be floating around in the clouds as a soul unencumbered by flesh and blood, or being reabsorbed into the mysterious energy field that binds everything together. That's The Force from <cite>Star Wars</cite>, by the way. But a disembodied future is not the Christian hope. Bodily resurrection matters. The resurrection of Christ matters. 

**Why does it matter whether Christ was truly raised from the dead?** We see three reasons in the text.  

1. First, because of the resurrection's centrality to the Christian faith. 
2. Second, because of the disastrous consequences of a dead Christ. 
3. Third, because of the glorious implications of a risen Christ.

## 1. The resurrection's centrality to the Christian faith (vv. 1–11)

**First, the resurrection of Christ matters because of its centrality to the Christian faith.** That's the point Paul makes in verses 1 to 11. Before even addressing the Corinthians' denial that the believing dead will be raised, he reaffirms the centrality of Christ's resurrection. It's foundational to Christianity.

### A gospel issue

**The resurrection is a gospel issue.** Paul begins by saying, "Now I would remind you, brothers, of the gospel I preached to you, which you received, in which you stand, and by which you are being saved." And then in verse 3, he says the gospel, at the center of which is the resurrection of Christ, is "of first importance." Did you hear that? It's of first importance. The resurrection of Christ isn't a minor detail that has no bearing on the truth claims of Christianity. It's an essential doctrine.

Maybe you've played the game Jenga. There's a tower of wooden blocks stacked on top of each other. Each player has to remove a single block and place it on top without causing the tower to fall over. Some blocks can be safely moved without a problem. But others are crucial. Move them and the game is over. 

The same is true of Christ's resurrection. Remove it from the gospel and the whole Christian faith collapses.

Belief in the resurrection of Christ is necessary for salvation. Look at verse 2. Paul says "you are being saved" by this gospel. "This gospel concerning Christ's resurrection, which I preached to you and which you received. Salvation depends on it." There are many in the broader church who would say they are Christian people. They claim to believe the gospel, but deny the literal, bodily resurrection of Christ. For them the gospel is an ethical system, a moral code: Love your neighbor as yourself. But that's not the gospel. The gospel isn't a set of rules to live by. It's an announcement of good news. News about what God has done to save sinners through the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

### The common and universal confession of the church

**Not only is the resurrection a gospel issue, it's the common and universal confession of the Apostles and the Christian church.** Christ's Apostles taught that he was raised from the dead. True churches, that is, churches that hold to the doctrine taught by the Apostles, have always confessed the resurrection.

Paul says in verse 3, "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received.</p> In other words, Paul didn't make it up. The doctrine of the resurrection didn't originate with him. He "received it"  and "delivered it" to the Corinthians. That's technical language for transmission of traditional material. The summary of the gospel in verses 3 to 5 is something that existed before Paul was on the scene as an Apostle.

Most scholars believe this summary was from an early Christian creed, something formulated within the first few years after the resurrection event. That means Christian churches were confessing their belief in the resurrection with this language 20 plus years before Paul wrote 1 Corinthians.

It's a beautiful statement of Christian belief. Four lines. Two nicely balanced sections. "He died and was buried. He was raised and appeared." That's the gospel in a nutshell. It's concise, but it communicates the essentials.

- Christ, that is, the Messiah, died for our sins, meaning he offered himself as a substitutionary sacrifice. 
- His burial verified that he truly had died. 
- He was raised on the third day. Notice the specificity. It was a real historical event, not a metaphor. 
- His death and resurrection fulfilled Old Testament promise and prophecy. Passages like Isaiah 53:5 come to mind: "But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed." Also, Psalm 22, Ezekiel 37, Hosea 6:2, and many others. 
- Not only was he raised; he appeared to many people after his resurrection.

Paul is reminding the Corinthians this is what all Christian churches worthy of the name believe. The resurrection is a key part of the gospel that all the Apostles preached. He says in verse 11, "Whether then it was I or they, so we preach and so you believed." Whether it was Peter or Paul or any other Apostle, there was one message: Christ died and was raised.

So, the person who professes to be a Christian but denies the bodily resurrection of Christ puts themself outside the bounds of orthodox, confessional Christianity. Christianity isn't whatever we want it to be. There's a system of doctrine that's been handed down to us from Christ's own Apostles. Our responsibility is to believe it, confess it, and proclaim it, not to tamper with it so it conforms to our modern sensibilities.

### It's true

**There's one other point I want to address here. Is it true? Did the resurrection really happen?** It's one thing to say it's an important doctrine and the church has always believed and taught the resurrection. But that doesn't make it true. Paul knows that, so he points to the witnesses. The resurrection is central to the Christian faith because it really happened.

There were eyewitness of the resurrected Christ. Paul lists a number of them beginning in verse 5. He appeared to Cephas, to Simon Peter. Then to the twelve disciples (minus Judas, of course). He appeared to more than five hundred people at one time (v. 6). And Paul adds, "most of whom are still alive." Why that detail? Paul's saying, "Look, you can go talk to the eyewitnesses. They'll tell you exactly what they saw with their own eyes. You can verify that what I'm saying is true."  If only one or two claimed to see the risen Christ, they could probably be ignored. But five hundred people at one time! Was that some mass hallucination? No. The only reasonable explanation is it really happened.

The list of witnesses continues in verse 7. The risen Christ also appeared to James, Jesus's half-brother. Then he appeared to all the apostles, perhaps a reference to the scene in Acts 1 before the ascension (1:6–11). "Last of all," verse 8, "as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me." The resurrection appearances ended with Paul on the road to Damascus.

You see his point here. There's reliable evidence for the resurrection. It's well attested by many different witnesses. Remember, 1 Corinthians was written just over two decades after Christ was raised. The eyewitness could be interviewed.

If you struggle to accept the resurrection as historical fact, consider the evidence. Belief in the resurrection doesn't require setting aside rational thought. If all we had were people who claimed to see the resurrected Christ, but the tomb wasn't empty, they could be dismissed as liars. It would've been easy to verify. If there was an empty tomb but no appearances, it could be assumed his disciples stole the body. And, of course, that was one of the earliest myths about the resurrection. Matthew deals with it in his Gospel.

The accounts of the resurrection in the Gospels are presented as eyewitness testimony. They're compelling. Our problem is we engage in what C.S. Lewis called <q cite="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronological_snobbery">chronological snobbery.</q> We think we're more advanced, more rational. We tell ourselves people in the ancient world thought supernatural events happened all the time. Of course Jesus's disciples didn't have trouble believing he had been raised. 

But if you read the Gospels, that's not what you see. They weren't expecting resurrection. When Jesus was placed in the tomb, their world collapsed. Their hopes and dreams disappeared. When the women first came and told the Apostles the tomb was empty and Christ had been raised, the men didn't believe it. (Luke 24:10). Thomas didn't believe it at first when the others told him they had seen the risen Christ.

How do you explain the transformation of Saul of Tarsus? He says here that he had persecuted the church (v. 9). No one expected Saul to become a Christian, least of all Saul himself. The only explanation is that he encountered the risen Christ on the road to Damascus. The grace of the living Christ transformed him into a preacher of the resurrection. Paul says, "It's true. Christ was raised, just ask the witnesses."

So, does the resurrection matter? Yes, it matters more than anything else. The Christian faith is built on the truth of the resurrection of Christ from the dead.

## 2. The disastrous consequences of a dead Christ (vv. 12–19)

Second, in verses 12 to 19 we see that the resurrection matters because of the disastrous consequences of a dead Christ.

What would we lose if Christ was still in the grave? Some would say not much. "We have Jesus's teaching and his example. They inspire people to do good in the world, so Christianity still has value without the resurrection." If you ask Paul what we lose, his answer is "everything."

He performs a little exercise with the Corinthians. It's as if he says, "Let's assume for the sake of argument that you're right: the dead are not raised. That being so, then Christ himself has not been raised. And if that's the case, what are the consequences?"

He lists five.

### The gospel and your faith

**First, consequence: the gospel message and your faith have no substance.** Verse 14: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain." What does he mean? The word he uses, vain, means "without content, without truth, lacking substance."

If the resurrection of Christ didn't happen, the gospel is empty. I should sit down right now. I might as well give up being a preacher. I've devoted my life to a myth. I left a good career in software to peddle nonsense. Of course, I don't believe that to be the case. But that's the consequence of a dead Christ. 

Why tell people Christ can be known, loved, and trusted; that he gives new life and forgives sin; that he promises to be with us always until we reach eternal glory, if in fact, he does none of those things because he's dead? If he didn't rise, he's just a rotted corpse in a tomb in Palestine.

If Christ has not been raised, your faith in him is nonsense. It's as useful as faith in the Easter Bunny. You should stop listening to this sermon if Christ is in the grave today. 

### The Apostles

**Second consequence: the Apostles are liars.** He goes on in 15, "We are even found to be misrepresenting God, because we testified about God that he raised Christ, whom he did not raise if it is true that the dead are not raised." If Christ has not been raised, then the Apostles are false witnesses about God. They told us God raised Christ, when, in fact, he didn't.

Do you see what Paul's saying? He can't be trusted. The Bible can't be trusted. It's full of lies if Christ didn't rise. Jesus of Nazareth was a fraud. He claimed to be the Son of God and said he would rise from the dead, but he didn't. If he didn't rise, we shouldn't admire his teaching; we should reject it as cruel and deceitful.

### Forgiveness of sins

**Third consequence: you aren't forgiven by God**. Verse 17: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins." Paul's saying your faith is a waste of time. It's worthless and fruitless if Christ is dead. Why? Because you're still in your sins. Your guilt hasn't been pardoned. You're still under God's judgment. If Christ wasn't raised, his death on the cross was no different than that of  the two criminals crucified on either side of him. 

No resurrection, no atonement. The two are linked. Romans 4:25 says, Jesus "was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification." How do you know the Father accepted Christ's sacrifice for sins? The proof is the resurrection. When you pay for groceries at the store, you're given a receipt. That piece of paper signifies that your payment was accepted. The resurrection of Christ was God's public declaration that he accepted Jesus's sacrifice of himself for our sins. But if he wasn't raised, he accomplished nothing by his death. God's justice against our sin hasn't been satisfied.

### The believing dead

**Fourth consequence: the believing dead are forever lost.** Paul says in verse 18, "Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ [in other words, those who have died trusting Christ], have perished." They've died under God's judgment because their sins weren't forgiven. There's no future of eternal blessedness for them. Instead, their lot is everlasting judgment and ruin.

Think about how different the funeral of a believer would be if Christ wasn't raised. A Christian's funeral is a time of grief because we've lost someone we love. But it's also suffused with hope because of the resurrection. Often the traditional words of committal are used at the graveside: 

> "In the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life through our Lord Jesus Christ, we commend to Almighty God our brother or sister; and we commit their body to the ground." 

We look forward to a happy reunion with them one day. But none of that is true if Christ has not been raised.

### You

**The fifth and final consequence: you are deluded and should be pitied.** Verse 19: "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied." 

When I was a new Christian I had a conversation with a friend who also had recently been converted. He was reflecting on the changes that had occurred in his life. He was excited by what he was seeing. He made a comment that at the time I probably agreed with. But now more than twenty years later I recognize it was confused thinking. He said, "If it turns out that Christianity isn't true, at least we've lived better lives."

Friends, that's not the case. If the resurrection isn't true, we've staked our lives on a lie. We're living as if some dead rabbi makes a difference in our lives now and hereafter. We're delusional.

Later in the chapter Paul points to his own life (vv. 30–32). "Consider all my suffering for Christ, all my sacrifice. It's been a colossal waste. Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die." Better to give up the faith now then to go on sacrificing and hoping for a tomorrow that's never going to come.

Do you see that without the resurrection of Christ we have nothing? No gospel. No truth. No hope or meaning. Christianity is built on the historical, bodily resurrection of Christ. It's useless if he's in the grave. 

Paul wants us to understand that the resurrection matters. It matters because of its centrality to the Christian faith. It matters because of the consequences of a dead Christ.

## 3. The glorious implications of a risen Christ (vv. 20–28)

Third, it matters because of the glorious implications of a risen Christ. Verse 20 begins, "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead." "Enough with the hypotheticals," Paul says. "Christ has been raised. It's a fact of history. And it's a fact of momentous implications for the future." 

Let me highlight two of those implications.

### Christ's resurrection and yours

**Number one: Christ's resurrection from the dead guarantees your resurrection.** That's what he says in verses 20 to 22. The risen Christ is the "firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep." It's an agricultural metaphor. Firstfruits were the sheaves that preceded the full harvest. They were a sign and a guarantee that more was to follow at a future time. Christ's resurrection from the dead around 30 A.D. was a pledge that you will be raised up one day. Because he was raised, you will most certainly be raised.

Why is that the case? The two resurrections --- Christ's and yours --- are inseparably linked. You have to understand that Christ wasn't raised as a private individual. He was raised as a public person --- the federal representative of all who belong to him by faith. What happens to him, happens to them.

Paul's draws this out in the comparison with Adam (vv. 21–22). "For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive." Adam was the head of the human race. He represented us. "In Adam's fall we sinned all."

Have you ever wondered why we die? A common answer is that it's just a fact of life, the outcome of a natural process. Now realize, that's not a Christian idea. Christianity says death isn't natural. It wasn't originally part of God's good creation. Physical death is God's judgment against sin. We die because we're born in Adam. We're condemned in him.

Death is inescapable for human beings, isn't it? Medical experts are doing their best right now to estimate how many Americans might die from coronavirus. They have to give ranges because they don't know the exact number. But here's something we do know: many who are infected with COVID-19 will survive, but they will still die one day. One out of every one human beings will die eventually. That's the grim reality of human existence. 

But Christ has come as the Second Adam. He came to undo and reverse the effects of the first Adam's sin. "As in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" (v. 22). All who are in Christ —— verse 23 adds, "all who belong to Christ” —— will be made alive. Not merely raised only to die again, like Lazarus. That wouldn't be the reversal of the curse. But raised to new creation life; a redeemed and glorified body free forever from all corruption and weakness and sin and death.

### The consummation of God's saving purposes

**Second implication: Christ's resurrection leads infallibly to the consummation of God's saving purposes.**

Beginning in verse 23 Paul traces out a chain of events whose terminus is the completion of Christ's mediatorial task. It's as if he's saying, "Look, if you only view the resurrection as an interesting miracle that happened to one man a long time ago, you're not seeing the big picture. You're trying to watch a video on your phone's tiny screen. Let me sit you down in front of a giant IMAX screen." 

There's more here than we have time to explore, but let me try to sketch for you the big picture.

First, verse 23: there's a divine order to the events. Christ was raised as the firstfruits --- the inauguration of the new creation. At his second coming, believers will be raised.

Next, verse 24: when the believing dead have been raised, the "end," the "telos," or the goal of history will have been reached. The Lord Jesus will hand over the kingdom to his Father. This is his mediatorial kingdom, not the eternal, universal dominion, which he, as the Second Person of the Trinity, has always shared with the Father and the Holy Spirit. But rather the kingdom given to him at his ascension to God's right hand as the Incarnate Son, our Mediator. Remember what Jesus said to his disciples after his resurrection: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (Matt 28:18). Why has it been given? So that he might bring his redemptive work to its consummation. The consummation comes, the text says, after he has destroyed all enemy opposition. 

Paul explains in verse 25: "For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet." Christ reigns now and there's a divine necessity to it. "He must reign." It's part of God's plan. Verse 26 goes on: "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" Christ's resurrection dealt the decisive blow. But death's final destruction is postponed until his people are raised bodily at his return.

We live in the gap. The time between the two resurrections --- Christ's and ours. Romans 8 says it's a time of groaning. We and the entire creation groan as we await the redemption of our bodies (Rom 8:19-23). 

Brothers and sisters, let every ache and pain, every cough and sniffle, and every disease remind you that it won't always be this way. Your physical body matters to God. It won't be discarded like a worthless piece of clothing. It'll be raised and made like Christ's glorious body (Phil 3:21). That's the consummation of his mediatorial work.

Finally, verse 28: "When all things are subjected to him, then the Son himself will also be subjected to him who put all things in subjection under him, that God may be all in all." The Son will deliver up the kingdom and subject himself to the One who put all things under his feet. Now understand, Paul's not thinking here of the Son strictly as the eternal, divine Son. But rather as the God-man, the Second Adam doing what the First Adam should have done.

And what's the result? God will be "all in all." This is the ultimate purpose of Christ's redeeming work: all enemies of God overthrown, a redeemed and glorified humanity living in perfect fellowship with God on a new earth, the divine glory and presence filling everything, and God forever being exalted in his glory as supreme over all.

## Conclusion

The resurrection of Christ matters! You are what your are as a Christian today because of the grace of the risen Christ. Your sins are forgiven. Your present and your future are secure. Why? Because you belong to the risen Christ.

History is headed toward an outburst of resurrection glory. Because Christ was raised, you will be raised with him. It will be a time of indescribable joy. A time of unhindered fellowship with the Triune God. A time of reunion with believers who preceded us in death. No more social distancing. No more sickness or sorrow.

The perishable will put on the imperishable. The mortal will put on immortality. "[T]hen shall come to pass the saying that is written: 'Death is swallowed up in victory.' 'O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?' The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law. But thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (vv. 54–57).

So, brothers and sisters, because of the sure and certain hope of the resurrection, "be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (v. 58).
